Modernizing Composite Workflows

Why traditional methods fall short — and how CATIA Composites Design solves it

Traditional composite workflows rely heavily on manual ply tracing, hand-cut templates, and disconnected CAD-to-simulation pipelines — introducing manufacturing inaccuracies, material waste, and inconsistent structural behaviour. By implementing the CATIA Composites Design workbench into the team workflow, composite layups can now be designed directly on CAD geometry while maintaining manufacturing accuracy and simulation fidelity throughout the entire process.

🔧 Core capabilities implemented: Precise ply/core placement on complex surfaces · Manufacturing-aware validation · Automated flat pattern generation · Streamlined CAD-to-ANSYS ACP export · Nesting-ready cut files.

Design Workflow

End-to-end digital composite development

The workflow begins by defining composite surfaces and manufacturing zones directly in CATIA. Plies, core regions, and fiber orientations are then created on the geometry while considering draping behaviour and manufacturability.

  • Creating ply books and stacking sequences
  • Assigning fiber orientations and materials
  • Designing core transition regions
  • Generating flat patterns for manufacturing
  • Exporting layups into ANSYS ACP for FEA validation
  • Generating splicing curves and flattened shapes
  • Nesting shapes onto fabric for precision cutting

A major improvement from this workflow was accurate flat pattern generation, introducing precise layup processes and minimal material waste from our nesting software.

Example Project — Canopy Layup

End-to-end design, splice planning, and producibility validation

The canopy project demonstrates the complete workflow: creating the ply schedule, generating cut shapes, and checking manufacturability with minimal material waste while ensuring structural integrity.

The layup was designed directly in the mold, including flanges and extra material for post-processing. Different colours represent distinct plies, depending on material type and fiber orientation.

Splicing curves define where material will be cut or where a ply terminates, limited by fabric roll width (60 inches in this case). Every cut piece must fit within the roll width to be manufacturable.

Fabric splice curves
Core splice curves

Producibility analysis checks draping feasibility for each cut piece. A completely blue result (as shown) indicates easy draping with no complex geometry issues. If red zones appear, additional splices or darts can relieve stress concentrations.

Flattened cut pieces — the final step generates exact 2D patterns that perfectly map onto the 3D mold surface when laid up. These pieces are then exported to nesting software and projected onto fabric for precision cutting.
